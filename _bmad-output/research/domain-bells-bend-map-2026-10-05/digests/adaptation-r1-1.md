## Scale options table

Assume a Bells Bend span of 4 to 6 km (from the brief, unverified) and a 5 m/s sprint. Walk speed is assumed at about 1.4 m/s; that is an unverified belief, not sourced this run.

A hybrid is worth noting: keep the vertical scale near 1:1 and compress the horizontal scale non-uniformly. Compress the open southern bottomland more (1:3) than the forested northern ridges (1:2). This mirrors "It's not the real Bolivia. We have to level design our world" (claim 5). Treat it as a design proposal, not a sourced practice.

## 25-year decay cheat sheet (1987 to about 2012, middle Tennessee)

**Former crop fields and pastures (southern bottomland):**
- **Not grassland any more.** By year 25 a field has passed annuals (years 0 to 2), then broomsedge (from year 3), then a shrub and cedar stage (from about year 5) (claim 6).
- Expect a **patchy young woodland of eastern redcedar 5.5 to 8 m tall**, trunks only 5 to 7.5 cm across, as dense dark-green cones. It may be closed-canopy where there was a nearby seed source (claim 7).
- In gaps there are **blackberry briar thickets, smooth sumac and broomsedge** remnants (claims 6 and 7).
- The Nashville Basin specifically favours persistent cedar on thin, rocky soils (claim 8). Deeper alluvial bottomland soils may shift faster to hardwoods. That is my belief; I did not find a source on floodplain succession speed.
- Sweetgum and tulip poplar as the 25-year hardwood entrants: **not evidenced this run** (FEIS didn't mention them). Treat this as a plausible belief.

**Edges, rights-of-way and fencelines:**
- **Kudzu blankets** road cuts, power-line corridors and field edges, climbing and smothering trees (claim 9).
- **Bush honeysuckle and privet** form dense, shady, near-impassable understorey thickets along forest edges and floodplains (claim 9). These make good natural walls and sight-blockers for level design.

**Asphalt roads:**
- Cracking within months. Over two decades: potholes and cratering, vegetation rooting in the cracks, and verges closing in so the carriageway narrows (claims 10, 11 and 12).
- After 25 years a rural two-lane road is likely a narrowed strip with grass and saplings in the cracks and shoulders, still traceable and walkable.

**Houses and barns (wood frame):**
- Water through failed roofs drives rot and mould. Within a couple of decades walls sag and roof struts splay (claims 10 and 11).
- After 25 years: a mix of sagging and partly collapsed roofs, trees in yards and some interiors, and animals nesting.
- Expect total roof loss on some buildings, though Weisman's "within 50" figure for roofs means many are still partly standing at 25 years.

**Bridges, steel and power lines:**
- Steel corrodes and fails where it's waterlogged (claim 10, NYC subway context).
- I found no source for a 25-year timeline for rural bridges, wooden utility poles or conductors. See **Could not find**.

**Real-world 25-year analog:** Pripyat, evacuated in 1986, which is about the same elapsed time as 1987 to 2012. Trees grow through windows, grass pushes through boulevard cracks, and summer vegetation closes the paths (claim 12).

## Unity tiling notes

- A single tile can be up to 100,000 m wide (claim 13), so size isn't the constraint. **Heightmap resolution per tile is**: 2^n+1, for example 1025 or 2049. For around 1 m height spacing over 4 to 6 km at 1:1, use several tiles (for example a 4x4 grid of 1.5 km tiles at 2049) rather than one huge tile.
  - The Unity docs page didn't list the maximum heightmap resolution; 4097 is my belief. Verify it.
- Put all tiles in one **Grouping ID** with Auto Connect so neighbours stitch LOD seams (claim 13).
- **Centre the world origin on the map centre.** Then max player offset is half the span: 2 to 3 km at 1:1, 1 to 1.5 km at 1:2 and about 1 km at 1:3. That keeps 1:2 and 1:3 inside the "safe" band and 1:1 in the "slight jitter" band (claim 14).
  - A floating origin is likely unnecessary below about 5 km offset. This is community guidance, not official Unity docs.
- Detail (grass) resolution goes up to 4048 per tile (claim 13). Dense old-field broomsedge and briar coverage over many km² argues for tile-based detail painting and aggressive detail distance.
